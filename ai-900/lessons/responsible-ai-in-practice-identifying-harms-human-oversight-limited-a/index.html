Microsoft Certified: Azure AI Fundamentals AI-900 · Domain 1: Describe AI workloads and considerations

Responsible AI in practice: identifying harms, human oversight, Limited Access features and transparency notes

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 25, 2026 · Leer en español

Principles only help if they change how systems are built and run. This lesson covers the practical tools and habits that put responsible AI into action, including some specific Microsoft mechanisms that appear on AI-900.

It starts with identifying potential harms early. Before building, teams ask who could be affected by the system and how: could it deny someone a service unfairly, expose personal data, give dangerous advice, or be misused for surveillance or deception? An impact assessment documents intended uses, stakeholders, possible harms and planned mitigations. Microsoft's Responsible AI Standard asks its own teams to complete one for AI systems.

Human oversight keeps a person involved where the stakes are high. Human-in-the-loop designs have a person approve AI recommendations before they take effect, such as a doctor confirming an AI-suggested diagnosis. Other designs let a human monitor the system and intervene, or let affected people appeal to a human reviewer. Showing confidence scores and routing low-confidence results to people are common patterns.

Limited Access is Microsoft's policy of restricting certain sensitive AI capabilities to approved customers and use cases. Customers must apply, describe their intended use and agree to terms before using features such as face identification and verification in Azure AI Face, or custom neural voice in Azure AI Speech, which can create synthetic voices resembling a real person. The aim is to reduce risks such as unlawful surveillance and impersonation. Microsoft also retired Face capabilities that inferred emotional states and identity attributes such as gender and age, because the science was unreliable and the potential for misuse was high.

Transparency notes and related documentation explain what each Azure AI service can and cannot do, how it was evaluated, and which uses are and are not recommended. Reading them is part of choosing a service responsibly. For generative AI, Microsoft also provides built-in content filtering, Azure AI Content Safety and evaluation tools, covered in domain 5.

Finally, responsible AI continues after launch: monitor performance and fairness over time, collect user feedback, log incidents, and be ready to roll back or switch off a system that causes harm.

Key terms

Impact assessment
A documented review of an AI system's intended uses, stakeholders, potential harms and mitigations.
Human in the loop
A design where a person reviews or approves AI outputs before they take effect.
Limited Access
Microsoft's policy that requires approval of the customer and use case before sensitive AI features can be used.
Custom neural voice
A Speech capability that creates a synthetic voice resembling a specific person; it is a Limited Access feature.
Real-world example

A hospital pilots an AI tool that prioritizes radiology scans. It completes an impact assessment, reads the vendor's transparency documentation, has radiologists confirm every urgent flag before action (human in the loop), and reviews missed cases monthly with a clinical safety officer.

Exam tip: If a question asks why face identification requires an application, the answer is Limited Access for responsible use, not pricing or preview status. Emotion, gender and age inference are no longer offered by Azure AI Face.

Check yourself

Name two Azure AI capabilities that fall under Limited Access.

Face identification and verification in Azure AI Face, and custom neural voice in Azure AI Speech.

What is the purpose of routing low-confidence predictions to a person?

Human oversight: people review uncertain cases so errors are caught before they cause harm.
