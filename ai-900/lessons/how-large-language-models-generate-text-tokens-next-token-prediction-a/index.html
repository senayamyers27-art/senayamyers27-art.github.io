How large language models generate text: tokens, next-token prediction and pretraining

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 25, 2026 · Leer en español

Large language models (LLMs) are the engines behind most generative AI. They are transformer-based neural networks with billions of parameters, trained on enormous amounts of text. Understanding how they produce text explains both what they are good at and why they sometimes get things wrong.

An LLM starts by tokenizing the prompt: splitting it into tokens, which may be words, parts of words or punctuation. Each token becomes an embedding vector, and the transformer layers use attention to build a representation of the whole prompt in context. The model then outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary being the next one.

Generation is next-token prediction repeated. The model picks a token from those probabilities (the choice is influenced by settings such as temperature), appends it to the text and predicts again, one token at a time, until it produces a stop signal or reaches the maximum length. A long answer is therefore many small predictions, each based on everything before it, including the model's own earlier output.

The model learns those probabilities during pretraining, a self-supervised process in which it reads vast amounts of text and learns to predict the next token. Doing this at huge scale teaches it grammar, facts, writing styles and patterns of reasoning. Many models are then further trained on instruction-following examples and human feedback so they behave as helpful assistants, respond to instructions and avoid harmful output. Organizations can also fine-tune some models on their own examples to specialize them, though prompting and grounding are usually tried first.

This design has consequences. A model knows only what was in its training data, up to a cutoff date, and nothing about your private documents unless you provide them in the prompt. It produces what is statistically plausible rather than looking facts up, so it can state false information fluently (often called hallucination). And each request has a limit on tokens (the context window) covering both the prompt and the response. Services typically bill by the number of input and output tokens.

Small language models (SLMs) apply the same ideas at smaller scale; they are cheaper and faster and can run on devices, with less broad capability. Choosing a model is a trade-off between capability, speed and cost.

Key terms

Large language model (LLM)
A very large transformer model trained on massive text data that generates language by predicting tokens.
Next-token prediction
Generating text by repeatedly predicting a likely next token and appending it.
Pretraining
Initial self-supervised training on large text collections that teaches a model language patterns and knowledge.
Context window
The maximum number of tokens a model can handle in one request, covering prompt and response.
Real-world example

Asked 'Write a haiku about autumn rain', a model tokenizes the prompt, predicts 'Soft', then 'drops', then 'on', and so on, each time choosing among likely tokens given everything so far. Asked about a company policy it never saw, it may invent a plausible answer, which is why grounding matters.

Exam tip: LLMs predict tokens; they do not look up stored answers. Anything about knowledge limits, cutoff dates or fabricated facts links back to this, and the usual fix is grounding with your own data.

Check yourself

Why might an LLM give a confident but wrong answer?

It generates statistically plausible tokens rather than retrieving verified facts, and may lack the relevant information in its training data.

What counts toward a model's context window?

The tokens in the prompt, including system message and any added data, plus the tokens in the response.
