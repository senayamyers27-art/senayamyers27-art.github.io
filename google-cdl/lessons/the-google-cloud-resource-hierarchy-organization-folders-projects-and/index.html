The Google Cloud resource hierarchy: organization, folders, projects and resources

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 25, 2026

Google Cloud organizes everything a company uses into a hierarchy. The hierarchy is the foundation for managing access, applying policies and tracking costs, so designing it well is one of the first steps in a cloud adoption.

At the top is the organization node, which represents the company. It is linked to the company's Cloud Identity or Google Workspace domain, such as example.com. Having an organization node gives central visibility and control over all projects, and it means projects belong to the company, not to individual employees who may leave.

Below it are folders, which group projects and other folders. Folders commonly represent departments (Finance, Marketing), teams, products or environments (Production, Development). Folders can be nested, which lets you mirror the company's structure and apply different policies to different groups.

Projects are the next level and the most important unit for day-to-day work. Every Google Cloud resource belongs to exactly one project. A project is where you enable APIs and services, it is linked to a billing account that pays for its usage, and it has its own IAM policies and quotas. Each project has a project name, a globally unique project ID that cannot be changed, and a project number. Using separate projects for different applications and environments keeps them isolated, simplifies access control and makes costs easy to see.

At the bottom are the resources themselves: VMs, Cloud Storage buckets, BigQuery datasets, Pub/Sub topics and so on.

Policies flow downward. An IAM grant or organization policy set on the organization applies to all folders, projects and resources below it; one set on a folder applies to everything in that folder. This is covered in the next lesson. A common good practice is to set broad guardrails high in the hierarchy and grant most access at the folder or project level.

Key terms

Organization node
The root of the resource hierarchy, representing the company.
Folder
A grouping of projects and other folders, often by department, team or environment.
Project
The basic unit for enabling services, billing, IAM and grouping resources; every resource belongs to one.
Project ID
A globally unique, permanent identifier for a project.
Real-world example

A retail company creates an organization node for example.com, folders for Stores, E-commerce and Corporate, and inside E-commerce separate projects for web-prod, web-dev and analytics. Each project links to the central billing account, and costs roll up clearly by folder.

Exam tip: Remember the order: organization, folders, projects, resources. Every resource lives in exactly one project, and projects are where APIs are enabled and billing is linked.

Check yourself

What is the correct order of the resource hierarchy from top to bottom?

Organization, folders, projects, resources.

Why use separate projects for production and development?

To isolate them, apply different access and policies, and see their costs separately.
