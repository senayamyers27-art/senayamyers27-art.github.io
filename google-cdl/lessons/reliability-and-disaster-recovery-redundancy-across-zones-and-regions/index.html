Reliability and disaster recovery: redundancy across zones and regions, backups, RTO and RPO

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 25, 2026 · Leer en español

Failures are inevitable: disks fail, software has bugs, people make mistakes and occasionally a whole data center or region has problems. Reliable systems are designed to keep working, or to recover quickly, when things go wrong. The cloud makes many reliability techniques easier and cheaper, but they still must be chosen and configured.

High availability (HA) is about keeping a service running through failures of individual components. The main technique is redundancy: running more than one instance so that others take over when one fails. In Google Cloud that means running instances in multiple zones of a region, for example with a regional managed instance group behind a load balancer, or using regional services such as a Cloud SQL instance configured for high availability, which fails over to a standby in another zone. Health checks and autohealing replace failed instances automatically.

Disaster recovery (DR) is about recovering from larger events, such as a regional outage, a major security incident or data corruption. Two metrics guide DR planning. The recovery time objective (RTO) is the maximum acceptable time a service can be unavailable before it must be restored. The recovery point objective (RPO) is the maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured as time: an RPO of 15 minutes means you must be able to restore data to within 15 minutes of the failure. Smaller RTOs and RPOs need more expensive designs.

DR patterns range from cheap to expensive. Backup and restore: regular backups stored in another region, with infrastructure rebuilt when needed; low cost but longer RTO and RPO. Warm standby: a scaled-down copy running in another region, ready to scale up. Hot or active-active: full capacity in multiple regions at once, with near-zero RTO and RPO but the highest cost.

Backups themselves must be protected, tested and ideally stored in a different location and account from production. A DR plan that has never been tested is only a hope, so organizations should rehearse recovery regularly.

Key terms

High availability
Designing a system to keep running despite component failures, usually through redundancy.
Disaster recovery
Plans and systems for restoring service after a major failure.
RTO
Recovery time objective: the maximum acceptable downtime before service is restored.
RPO
Recovery point objective: the maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time.
Real-world example

An online retailer runs its store across three zones in one region for high availability. For disaster recovery it replicates its database to a second region and keeps a small warm standby there, meeting its RTO of one hour and RPO of five minutes. It tests failover twice a year.

Exam tip: RPO is about data loss (how far back you restore to); RTO is about downtime (how long until you are running). Multiple zones protect against zone failure; multiple regions protect against regional disasters.

Check yourself

A business can lose at most 10 minutes of data. Which metric is this?

The recovery point objective (RPO).

Which DR pattern gives the lowest RTO and RPO, and what is its drawback?

Active-active (hot) across multiple regions; it is the most expensive.
