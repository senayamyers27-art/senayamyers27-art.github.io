Identity and access management: principals, roles, least privilege and two-step verification

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 25, 2026 · Leer en español

Identity and access management (IAM) answers the question: who can do what on which resource? In the cloud, where resources are reached over the internet, IAM is one of the most important security controls, and misused access is behind many incidents.

In Google Cloud IAM, a principal is the identity that receives access. Principals include Google accounts for individual users, Google groups, service accounts (identities used by applications and VMs rather than people), and Cloud Identity or Google Workspace domains. Managing access through groups rather than individuals makes it easier to add and remove people as their jobs change. Organizations often manage user accounts with Cloud Identity or Google Workspace and can sync them from existing directories.

Permissions allow specific actions, such as starting a VM or reading a storage object. Permissions are not granted directly; they are bundled into roles, and roles are granted to principals on a resource through an allow policy. Basic roles (Owner, Editor, Viewer) are broad and apply across all services in a project; they are not recommended for most production use. Predefined roles are created by Google for specific jobs on specific services, such as Storage Object Viewer or Compute Instance Admin. Custom roles let you define exactly the permissions you need.

The principle of least privilege means giving each principal only the permissions needed for its task, on the smallest scope, for no longer than needed. In practice: prefer predefined or custom roles over basic roles, grant at the project or resource level rather than the organization when possible, use groups, review access regularly, and act on IAM recommendations that point out unused permissions. Separation of duties, where no single person controls a whole sensitive process, complements it.

Authentication also matters. Two-step verification (2SV), also called multi-factor authentication, requires a second factor in addition to a password, such as a code from an app or, better, a physical security key. Security keys resist phishing because they only work with the genuine site. Administrators should always use strong 2SV.

Key terms

Principal
An identity that can be granted access, such as a user, group or service account.
Role
A collection of permissions that can be granted to a principal on a resource.
Least privilege
Granting only the permissions needed, on the narrowest scope, for only as long as needed.
Service account
An identity used by applications and workloads rather than people.
Real-world example

A company replaces the Editor role it had given all developers with a group that has predefined roles for the specific services they use, in development projects only. Production access is granted to a small operations group, and every administrator uses a security key for 2SV.

Exam tip: Grant roles, not permissions, to groups rather than individuals, on the smallest scope. Basic roles (Owner, Editor, Viewer) are almost never the least-privilege answer.

Check yourself

What are the three kinds of IAM roles in Google Cloud?

Basic (Owner, Editor, Viewer), predefined, and custom roles.

Why are security keys better than SMS codes for 2SV?

Security keys resist phishing because they only authenticate to the genuine site, while codes can be tricked out of users.
