Core security concepts: confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy, control and compliance

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 25, 2026

Security discussions use a small set of core terms, and the Cloud Digital Leader exam expects you to recognize them in business scenarios. Three of them form the classic CIA triad.

Confidentiality means information is accessible only to those authorized to see it. A leaked customer database or an exposed storage bucket is a confidentiality failure. Controls include access management, encryption and data classification. Integrity means information is accurate and has not been altered without authorization. A tampered payment record or a changed configuration file is an integrity failure. Controls include access control, audit logs, checksums and digital signatures. Availability means systems and data are accessible when authorized users need them. A denial-of-service attack, a regional outage or ransomware that locks files are availability failures. Controls include redundancy across zones and regions, backups, DDoS protection and disaster recovery plans.

Privacy is related to confidentiality but focuses on personal information and the rights of the people it describes: what is collected, why, how it is used, who it is shared with and how long it is kept. Privacy is governed by laws such as the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Security is necessary for privacy but not enough: data can be perfectly secure and still be used in ways that break privacy rules.

Control refers to the customer's ability to decide how its data and systems are managed in the cloud: where data is stored, who can access it, who holds the encryption keys and whether the provider's staff can access it. Google Cloud offers features such as customer-managed keys, data location choices and Access Transparency to give customers more control.

Compliance means meeting the requirements of laws, regulations, industry standards and contracts, such as GDPR, HIPAA in US healthcare or PCI DSS for card payments. Cloud providers are audited against many standards, but customers are still responsible for using services in a compliant way. Compliance is not the same as security: a compliant organization can still be breached, so compliance is a minimum rather than a goal.

Key terms

Confidentiality
Ensuring information is only accessible to authorized people.
Integrity
Ensuring information is accurate and not altered without authorization.
Availability
Ensuring systems and data are accessible when needed.
Compliance
Meeting the requirements of laws, regulations, standards and contracts.
Real-world example

A payment processor encrypts card data and limits access (confidentiality), logs and signs every transaction record (integrity), runs across multiple zones with DDoS protection (availability), and undergoes a yearly PCI DSS assessment (compliance).

Exam tip: Map the incident to the property: data seen by the wrong people is confidentiality, data changed is integrity, service down is availability. Being compliant does not mean being secure.

Check yourself

Ransomware encrypts a company's files so staff cannot use them. Which CIA property is mainly affected?

Availability (and possibly confidentiality if data was also stolen).

How does privacy differ from security?

Privacy concerns how personal data is collected and used and people's rights over it; security protects data from unauthorized access or change.
