ISACA Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA) 2024 job practice · Domain 1: Information system auditing process

Types of controls: preventive, detective, corrective, compensating; general vs application controls

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

A control is anything management puts in place to give reasonable assurance that objectives are met and risks are kept acceptable. Auditors classify controls so they can judge whether the mix is sensible and decide how to test each one.

By function, preventive controls stop a problem before it happens: segregation of duties, input validation, access controls, approvals. Detective controls find problems after they occur: reconciliations, log reviews, exception reports, hash totals. Corrective controls fix the problem and reduce its impact: backup restoration, incident response, contingency plans. Deterrent controls discourage bad behavior, such as warning banners, and directive controls tell people what to do, such as policies. A compensating control is an alternative that reduces risk when the ideal control is not practical, for example supervisory review of logs when a small team cannot fully segregate duties.

By scope, IT general controls (ITGCs) apply across many systems: logical access, change management, IT operations, backup and recovery, and system development. Application controls are built into a specific application and cover input, processing and output, such as edit checks, calculation checks and output reconciliation. Application controls can only be relied on if the general controls around them work. If anyone can change the program code without approval, a perfect edit check today may be gone tomorrow.

Controls can also be manual or automated. Automated controls are consistent and, once tested and protected by good change management, can often be tested with a small sample or a single test of one instance. Manual controls depend on people, vary more, and need larger samples.

Key terms

Compensating control
An alternative control that reduces risk to an acceptable level when the primary control cannot be implemented.
IT general control (ITGC)
A control over the IT environment, such as access or change management, that supports all applications running in it.
Application control
A control inside a specific application that ensures complete, accurate and authorized input, processing and output.
Detective control
A control that identifies errors or irregularities after they have occurred.
Real-world example

A payroll system has an automated check that rejects salaries above a limit. The auditor first tests change management and access to the program library; only after confirming nobody can alter the check without approval does she rely on one test of the automated control.

Exam tip: Weak general controls undermine application controls. If a question asks what to test first, IT general controls such as change management usually come before relying on automated application controls.

Check yourself

Classify a daily reconciliation of bank transactions to the ledger.

Detective, because it identifies differences after processing has occurred.

Why can an automated control often be tested with a very small sample?

It performs the same way every time, provided general controls such as change management keep it from being altered.
