Business case and feasibility analysis

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

Before an organization spends money on a new system, it should understand why. The business case documents the problem or opportunity, the options considered, the costs, benefits and risks of each, and the recommended solution. It is the reference point for deciding whether to start, continue or stop a project.

Feasibility analysis examines whether a solution is practical from several angles. Technical feasibility asks whether the technology exists and fits the environment. Economic feasibility compares costs with benefits using measures such as return on investment, net present value, payback period and total cost of ownership, which includes ongoing support, licenses and training, not only purchase price. Operational feasibility asks whether people and processes can adopt it. Legal and compliance feasibility checks regulatory constraints. Schedule feasibility considers whether it can be delivered when needed.

Options usually include doing nothing, improving the current system, buying a commercial product, using a cloud service or building a custom system. A buy decision leads to a structured selection: defining requirements, issuing a request for proposal, evaluating vendors against weighted criteria, checking references and financial stability, and negotiating contracts. A build decision leads into a development methodology.

The business case is not a one-time document. It should be revisited at major milestones and whenever costs, benefits or risks change significantly. If a project's justification no longer holds, stopping it is a legitimate outcome; money already spent is a sunk cost and should not by itself justify continuing. After implementation, the post-implementation review checks whether the promised benefits were realized.

Key terms

Business case
A document that justifies an investment by comparing options, costs, benefits and risks.
Total cost of ownership (TCO)
All costs of a solution over its life, including acquisition, operation, support and retirement.
Feasibility study
An analysis of whether a proposed solution is technically, economically, operationally and legally practical.
Sunk cost
Money already spent that cannot be recovered and should not drive future decisions.
Real-world example

A hospital compares upgrading its old scheduling system, buying a commercial product and subscribing to a cloud service. The business case shows the cloud option has the lowest five-year total cost of ownership and meets privacy requirements, so the steering committee approves it with a review at the end of the pilot.

Exam tip: When a project's costs rise or benefits fall, the best answer is usually to re-evaluate the business case, not to continue because of money already spent.

Check yourself

Name three types of feasibility.

Any three of: technical, economic, operational, legal or compliance, and schedule.

When should a business case be reviewed after approval?

At key milestones, when significant changes occur, and in the post-implementation review.
