ISACA Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA) 2024 job practice · Domain 1: Information system auditing process

Risk-based audit planning: audit universe, risk assessment, annual plan and engagement scope

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

No audit function can review everything every year. Risk-based audit planning is how you decide where limited audit hours will do the most good. ISACA's standards require the auditor to use a risk-based approach, and the exam rewards answers that start with understanding the business and its risks.

Planning happens at two levels. At the annual level, the audit function lists its audit universe, meaning every auditable area such as business processes, applications, infrastructure and third parties. It then rates each area for risk, considering factors like financial impact, regulatory exposure, complexity, recent changes, past findings and management's own risk assessments. The highest-risk areas get audited more often and more deeply. The resulting annual plan is approved by the audit committee and revised when risks change.

At the engagement level, the auditor first gains an understanding of the business process, its objectives, systems and key risks. From that, the auditor sets the audit objectives and scope, identifies key controls, and decides the nature, timing and extent of testing. This is where the audit risk model helps. Inherent risk is the risk before any controls. Control risk is the chance controls will not prevent or detect an error. Detection risk is the chance the auditor's own procedures miss it. The auditor can only change detection risk, by doing more or better testing when inherent and control risks are high.

Materiality also shapes planning. In IS audit, materiality is not only a dollar threshold; a weakness can be material because of what it could allow, such as a flaw that exposes sensitive data or lets someone bypass approvals across all transactions.

Key terms

Audit universe
The complete list of auditable areas in the organization, used as the starting point for risk-based planning.
Inherent risk
The risk of an error or loss before considering any controls.
Detection risk
The risk that the auditor's procedures fail to detect a material error; the only part of audit risk the auditor controls.
Materiality
The significance of a weakness or error, judged by its potential effect on decisions or objectives, not only its dollar value.
Real-world example

A bank's audit team scores 60 auditable areas. The new mobile banking platform scores high for customer data exposure, recent launch and regulatory attention, so it is scheduled for this year with a deep scope; a stable internal wiki scores low and is placed on a three-year cycle.

Exam tip: If asked what the auditor should do first when planning, choose understanding the business and assessing risk. Choosing tests or tools comes later.

Check yourself

Which component of audit risk can the auditor reduce directly?

Detection risk, by changing the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures.

Why can a small-dollar IT weakness still be material?

Because of what it could enable, such as unauthorized access to all customer records or bypassing controls on every transaction.
