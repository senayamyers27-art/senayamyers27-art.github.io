Enterprise architecture and enterprise risk management (ERM)

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

Enterprise architecture (EA) is the blueprint of how an organization's business processes, information, applications and technology fit together, both today and in the target future state. It helps leaders see duplication, gaps and dependencies so that investments move the organization toward a coherent design instead of adding disconnected systems.

EA frameworks such as TOGAF or the Zachman Framework describe the enterprise from several views: business, data, application and technology. A typical EA effort documents the current state, defines the target state, and produces a roadmap of projects to close the gap. For auditors, EA matters because well-governed architecture reduces complexity, which reduces risk, and because architecture decisions, such as which systems hold sensitive data and how they connect, shape where controls are needed.

Enterprise risk management (ERM) is the organization-wide process of identifying, assessing, responding to and monitoring risks to objectives. IT risk is one category within ERM, alongside financial, operational, strategic and compliance risk. Key ideas include risk appetite (how much risk the organization is willing to accept to achieve its goals), risk tolerance (acceptable variation around objectives), risk owners, risk registers, and the four responses: mitigate, transfer, avoid or accept. Residual risk remains after controls and must be within appetite or formally accepted by an owner with authority.

The auditor evaluates whether IT risks are identified and assessed consistently with ERM, whether owners are assigned, whether responses are implemented and whether reporting reaches the board. The auditor does not own or accept risks.

Key terms

Enterprise architecture
A structured description of business processes, information, applications and technology and how they should evolve.
Risk appetite
The amount and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain to achieve its objectives.
Residual risk
The risk remaining after controls and other responses are applied.
Risk owner
The person accountable for managing a particular risk and deciding on its response.
Real-world example

A merger leaves a company with three customer databases. The enterprise architecture team defines a target state with one customer master, and the ERM register records data inconsistency and privacy exposure as risks owned by the chief operating officer until consolidation is done.

Exam tip: Risk acceptance belongs to a business owner with authority, within the risk appetite. Auditors identify and report risks; they never accept them.

Check yourself

Name the four risk responses.

Mitigate (reduce), transfer (share), avoid and accept.

How does enterprise architecture reduce risk?

By reducing complexity and duplication and making dependencies visible, so controls can be placed where they are needed.
