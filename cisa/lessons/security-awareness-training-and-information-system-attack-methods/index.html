ISACA Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA) 2024 job practice · Domain 5: Protection of information assets

Security awareness training and information system attack methods

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

People are both a target and a defense. Many attacks start by tricking someone, and trained staff who spot and report attempts stop many of them. Understanding how attacks work helps the auditor judge whether controls address real threats.

Social engineering manipulates people. Phishing sends deceptive emails to steal credentials or deliver malware; spear phishing targets specific people; whaling targets executives; vishing and smishing use voice calls and text messages. Business email compromise uses spoofed or hijacked executive or supplier email to request payments or data. Pretexting invents a story to obtain information, and tailgating exploits courtesy to enter secure areas.

Technical attacks include malware such as ransomware, which encrypts data and demands payment; trojans and worms; password attacks such as credential stuffing and brute force; denial-of-service attacks that overwhelm systems; on-path (man-in-the-middle) attacks that intercept communications; and web application attacks such as SQL injection and cross-site scripting that exploit poor input handling. Advanced persistent threats are well-resourced attackers who stay hidden in a network for long periods. Insider threats come from employees or contractors, whether malicious or careless. Supply chain attacks compromise a trusted vendor or software update to reach its customers.

A security awareness program gives all staff regular, role-appropriate training, with extra training for high-risk roles such as finance and administrators. It uses phishing simulations, clear ways to report suspicious messages and messages from leadership. Effectiveness is measured by behavior, such as simulation click and report rates over time and reported incidents, not just training completion. Process controls, such as call-back verification for payment changes, back up training.

Key terms

Phishing
Deceptive messages that try to trick recipients into revealing information or running malicious content.
Business email compromise (BEC)
Fraud using spoofed or compromised business email to trick staff into sending money or data.
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts or steals data and demands payment for its release.
SQL injection
An attack that inserts malicious database commands through poorly validated input.
Real-world example

An accounts payable clerk receives an email from a supplier's real address, compromised by an attacker, asking to change bank details. Following training and procedure, she calls the supplier on the number already on file, discovers the fraud and reports it to security.

Exam tip: Measure awareness by behavior change, such as phishing simulation click and report trends, rather than attendance. For payment fraud, independent call-back verification is a strong process control.

Check yourself

What distinguishes spear phishing from ordinary phishing?

It targets specific individuals or groups with tailored, convincing content.

Which control best counters business email compromise requests to change bank details?

Verifying the request through an independent, known channel such as a call-back to a number already on file, plus dual approval.
