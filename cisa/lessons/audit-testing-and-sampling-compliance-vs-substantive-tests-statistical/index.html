ISACA Certified Information Systems Auditor (CISA) 2024 job practice · Domain 1: Information system auditing process

Audit testing and sampling: compliance vs substantive tests, statistical and non-statistical sampling

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

Auditors rarely test every item, so they need to know which kind of test answers their question and how to pick a sample they can defend. The exam tests both ideas regularly.

A compliance test (also called a test of controls) checks whether a control is operating as designed, such as whether sampled changes had approval before deployment. A substantive test checks the data itself for errors or misstatement, such as recalculating interest or confirming balances. The two are linked: when compliance testing shows controls are strong, less substantive testing is needed; when controls are weak, the auditor extends substantive testing to find out whether errors actually occurred.

Sampling can be statistical or non-statistical. Statistical sampling uses random or systematic selection and probability math so the auditor can state confidence and project results to the population. Non-statistical (judgmental) sampling relies on auditor judgment, for example choosing all high-value items; it is faster but cannot measure sampling risk objectively. Attribute sampling estimates a rate of occurrence, which suits compliance testing. Variable sampling estimates a quantity such as a dollar amount, which suits substantive testing; monetary unit sampling is a common form. Stop-or-go sampling lets the auditor stop early if few errors appear, and discovery sampling is used to find at least one instance of a rare event such as fraud.

Key terms appear often. The confidence coefficient is how sure you want to be; higher confidence needs a larger sample. The tolerable error rate is the maximum rate you can accept; a lower tolerable rate needs a larger sample. The expected error rate also matters: expecting more errors requires more samples. Stratification splits a population into groups, such as by amount, so each is sampled appropriately.

Key terms

Compliance test
A test of whether a control operates as designed, such as checking approvals on a sample of changes.
Substantive test
A test of the data or transactions themselves to detect errors or misstatement.
Attribute sampling
A method that estimates the rate at which a characteristic, such as a control failure, occurs in a population.
Tolerable error rate
The maximum rate of deviation the auditor is willing to accept and still rely on the control.
Real-world example

An auditor picks 40 random user-access requests from a year of 3,000 and checks each for manager approval. Two lack approval, giving a 5% deviation rate against a tolerable rate of 3%, so she concludes the control is not reliable and extends substantive testing of actual access rights.

Exam tip: Match the method to the question: attribute sampling for how often a control fails, variable or monetary unit sampling for how much money is wrong, discovery sampling for proving a rare event exists.

Check yourself

What happens to sample size if the auditor wants higher confidence?

It increases; higher confidence requires a larger sample.

If compliance tests show a control is weak, what should the auditor do?

Increase substantive testing to determine whether errors have actually occurred.
