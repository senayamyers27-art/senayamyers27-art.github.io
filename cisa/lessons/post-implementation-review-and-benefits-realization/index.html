Post-implementation review and benefits realization

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

A project is not truly finished when the system goes live. The post-implementation review (PIR) asks whether the project achieved what it set out to do and what the organization can learn. It closes the loop with the business case.

A PIR is typically performed after the system has been in production long enough for results to be measured, often several months. It compares actual costs, benefits and schedule with the business case; checks whether user requirements are met and users are satisfied; checks whether controls are working as designed, including security, audit trails and access; reviews outstanding defects and change requests; and captures lessons learned for future projects. It may be performed by the project team, quality assurance or an independent party; an auditor may participate or review it.

Benefits realization is the discipline of planning, tracking and confirming the benefits promised in the business case. Benefits should be defined in measurable terms with owners and target dates, such as reducing processing time by 30% within a year. Benefits often arrive after the project team has disbanded, so business owners must be accountable for delivering them. If benefits are not being realized, the organization should understand why and decide whether further action is needed.

For auditors, the PIR is also a source of evidence about the project's control environment. A missing PIR is itself a finding, because the organization cannot tell whether its investment paid off or learn from mistakes.

Key terms

Post-implementation review (PIR)
A review after go-live that compares actual results with the business case and captures lessons learned.
Benefits realization
Managing and tracking the delivery of the benefits promised by an investment.
Lessons learned
Documented insights from a project that help improve future projects.
Benefit owner
The business person accountable for achieving a specific benefit after implementation.
Real-world example

Six months after launching a self-service portal, the PIR finds that call center volume fell by 18% instead of the promised 30%, because two common requests were not included. The benefit owner adds them to the backlog and the lessons learned note that call data should shape requirements.

Exam tip: The PIR happens after the system has run long enough to measure benefits, not right after go-live and not before. Its main reference point is the business case.

Check yourself

What document does a PIR compare results against?

The business case, including its costs, benefits and schedule.

Who should be accountable for benefits after a project closes?

Named business benefit owners.
