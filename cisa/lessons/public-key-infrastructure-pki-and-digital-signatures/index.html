Public key infrastructure (PKI) and digital signatures

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

Asymmetric cryptography needs a way to trust that a public key really belongs to the person or system it claims. Public key infrastructure (PKI) provides that trust through digital certificates issued by trusted certificate authorities.

A digital certificate binds a public key to an identity, such as a website name or a person, and is signed by a certificate authority (CA). It contains the subject, the public key, the issuer, validity dates, a serial number and allowed key uses. A registration authority (RA) verifies identities before the CA issues certificates. Root CAs sit at the top of the trust chain, usually kept offline, and issuing CAs below them sign day-to-day certificates. Certificates can be revoked before they expire, for example when a private key is compromised, and relying parties check status through certificate revocation lists (CRLs) or the online certificate status protocol (OCSP). A certificate practice statement describes how the CA operates.

Digital signatures provide integrity, authentication and non-repudiation. The signer hashes the message and encrypts the hash with their private key. The recipient decrypts the signature with the signer's public key and compares it with a fresh hash of the message. A match proves the message was not changed and that only the private key holder could have signed it. Confidentiality is a separate service: to keep a message secret, encrypt it with the recipient's public key (in practice, a symmetric key protected that way).

Private keys must be protected, ideally in hardware tokens or HSMs. The auditor reviews CA controls, certificate inventories and expiry monitoring, revocation processes and private key protection. Expired certificates causing outages are a common operational finding.

Key terms

Certificate authority (CA)
A trusted entity that issues and signs digital certificates.
Digital signature
A hash of a message encrypted with the signer's private key, providing integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.
Certificate revocation list (CRL)
A list published by a CA of certificates revoked before their expiry.
Non-repudiation
Assurance that a party cannot credibly deny having sent or signed a message.
Real-world example

A company signs electronic purchase orders with employees' private keys stored on smart cards. When a supplier disputes an order, the company verifies the signature with the employee's certificate, proving the order came from that card and was not altered.

Exam tip: Sign with your private key; verify with the sender's public key. Encrypt for confidentiality with the recipient's public key. Symmetric keys cannot give non-repudiation because both sides hold them.

Check yourself

Which key verifies a digital signature?

The signer's public key.

When should a certificate be revoked?

When its private key is compromised, the holder's details change or it is no longer needed before expiry.
