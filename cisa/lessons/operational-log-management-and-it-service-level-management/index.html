Operational log management and IT service level management

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

Logs are the memory of IT operations. They record what systems did, who did it and when, supporting troubleshooting, performance analysis, security monitoring, investigations and audit. Logs only help if the right events are captured, kept long enough, protected and actually reviewed.

Log management covers deciding what to log based on risk and requirements (for example logins, privilege use, configuration changes and errors), synchronizing clocks so events can be correlated, sending logs to a central store that administrators cannot alter, retaining them for the period required by policy or regulation, and reviewing them regularly or through automated alerts. Logs may contain sensitive data, so access to them must be controlled. A common weakness is local logs that overwrite themselves in days, so evidence disappears before anyone looks.

IT service level management defines, agrees and monitors the level of service IT provides. A service catalog describes available services. Service level agreements (SLAs) set measurable targets with customers, such as availability, response time and incident resolution time. Operational level agreements (OLAs) are internal agreements between IT teams that support the SLA, and underpinning contracts bind external suppliers to matching commitments. Targets should be realistic, measurable and linked to business needs.

Service level management includes regular reporting of actual performance against targets, reviews with customers, and improvement plans when targets are missed. The auditor checks that SLAs exist for critical services, that performance data comes from reliable monitoring rather than self-reporting, and that supplier contracts support the SLAs.

Key terms

Service level agreement (SLA)
An agreement between a service provider and customer that defines measurable service targets.
Operational level agreement (OLA)
An internal agreement between IT teams supporting the delivery of an SLA.
Log retention
How long logs are kept, set by policy, legal requirements and investigation needs.
Time synchronization
Keeping system clocks aligned, for example with NTP, so events can be correlated across systems.
Real-world example

An SLA promises 99.9% monthly availability for online banking. The auditor compares the provider's monthly reports with independent monitoring data and finds two outages missing from the reports, so reported performance cannot be relied on.

Exam tip: The best evidence of SLA performance is reliable, independent measurement compared with targets. For logs, the key concerns are completeness, protection from alteration, adequate retention and actual review.

Check yourself

What is an operational level agreement?

An internal agreement between IT teams that supports an SLA with the customer.

Why send logs to a central store?

To protect them from alteration or deletion on the source system, keep them longer and allow correlation and review.
