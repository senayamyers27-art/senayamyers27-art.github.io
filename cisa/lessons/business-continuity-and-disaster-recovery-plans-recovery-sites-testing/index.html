Business continuity and disaster recovery plans: recovery sites, testing and maintenance

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed September 29, 2026 · Leer en español

A business continuity plan (BCP) describes how the organization keeps critical business processes going during and after a disruption. A disaster recovery plan (DRP) is the IT part: how systems, data and infrastructure are restored. Both depend on the BIA and on senior management support.

Plans should define scope and assumptions, roles and responsibilities, activation criteria and who declares a disaster, communication plans for staff, customers, regulators and media, contact lists, recovery procedures in priority order, and arrangements with suppliers. Copies must be available when primary systems and buildings are not. Teams usually include incident management, damage assessment, recovery, and business resumption roles.

Recovery site options balance cost and speed. A hot site is fully equipped with current data and can take over within hours. A warm site has some equipment and connectivity but needs data restored and configuration, taking days. A cold site has only space, power and cooling, taking weeks. Mirrored sites run in parallel with near-zero downtime at high cost. Cloud-based disaster recovery can provide hot or warm capacity on demand. Reciprocal agreements with other organizations are cheap but hard to enforce and test.

Testing proves the plan works and trains people. In increasing order of realism and disruption, tests include checklist reviews, tabletop or walk-through exercises, simulations, parallel tests (bringing up recovery systems without stopping production) and full interruption tests (actually shutting down primary operations). Results are documented, gaps fixed and plans retested. Plans must be maintained after organizational, system and staff changes and reviewed at least annually. An untested or outdated plan is one of the most common audit findings.

Key terms

Hot site
A fully equipped recovery site with current data that can resume operations within hours.
Cold site
A recovery facility with space and utilities but no equipment or data.
Tabletop exercise
A discussion-based test where participants walk through a scenario to check their understanding of the plan.
Full interruption test
A test that shuts down primary operations and runs entirely at the recovery site; the most realistic and most disruptive.
Real-world example

An insurer's DR test restores its policy system at a warm site but takes 30 hours against a 12-hour RTO because network changes were missing from the plan. The team updates the runbook, adds pre-staged network configuration and passes a retest within 10 hours.

Exam tip: The BIA comes first, then strategy, plan, testing and maintenance. After a failed test, update the plan and retest; after major changes, update the plan.

Check yourself

Which test is the most disruptive?

A full interruption test, because it actually stops primary operations.

Why are reciprocal agreements weak recovery options?

The partner may lack capacity when needed, they are hard to enforce and they are rarely tested.
