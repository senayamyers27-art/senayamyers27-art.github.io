Domain 1: Information system auditing process (18%)

Exam tips

  • When a question involves an independence problem, the answer is almost always to disclose it to audit management or the audit committee, not to carry on or to narrow the scope quietly.
  • Control self-assessment does not replace audit. If an answer suggests CSA removes the need for independent testing, it is wrong; CSA supplements audit and improves ownership.
  • If asked what the auditor should do first when planning, choose understanding the business and assessing risk. Choosing tests or tools comes later.
  • Weak general controls undermine application controls. If a question asks what to test first, IT general controls such as change management usually come before relying on automated application controls.
  • If an auditee refuses access or restricts scope, the right answer is to document it and escalate through audit management, not to drop the objective or proceed as if nothing happened.
  • Match the method to the question: attribute sampling for how often a control fails, variable or monetary unit sampling for how much money is wrong, discovery sampling for proving a rare event exists.
  • When ranking evidence, prefer independent over internal, auditor-obtained over auditee-provided, and documentary or reperformed over oral statements.
  • Before trusting any analytics result, verify the completeness and accuracy of the extracted data. And remember that continuous monitoring belongs to management, while continuous auditing belongs to audit.
  • Auditors report and verify; they do not fix. Any option where the auditor implements the corrective action or quietly drops a supported finding is wrong.

Key terms

ITAF
ISACA's IT Audit Framework: mandatory standards, supporting guidelines, and tools and techniques for IS audit and assurance work.
Independence in appearance
The absence of circumstances that would lead a reasonable third party to doubt the auditor's objectivity.
Due professional care
Applying the skill and diligence that a prudent, competent auditor would use in the same circumstances.
Self-review threat
The risk that an auditor will not critically evaluate work they performed or designed themselves.
Integrated audit
An audit that combines financial, operational and IS audit work to evaluate all the controls over a process or system together.
Control self-assessment (CSA)
A technique in which process owners and staff evaluate their own controls, usually in facilitated workshops.
Forensic audit
An engagement to collect and analyze evidence about suspected fraud or crime for possible legal or disciplinary use.
Compliance audit
An audit that tests adherence to specific laws, regulations, contracts or internal policies.
Audit universe
The complete list of auditable areas in the organization, used as the starting point for risk-based planning.
Inherent risk
The risk of an error or loss before considering any controls.
Detection risk
The risk that the auditor's procedures fail to detect a material error; the only part of audit risk the auditor controls.
Materiality
The significance of a weakness or error, judged by its potential effect on decisions or objectives, not only its dollar value.
Compensating control
An alternative control that reduces risk to an acceptable level when the primary control cannot be implemented.
IT general control (ITGC)
A control over the IT environment, such as access or change management, that supports all applications running in it.
Application control
A control inside a specific application that ensures complete, accurate and authorized input, processing and output.
Detective control
A control that identifies errors or irregularities after they have occurred.
Audit program
A step-by-step list of audit procedures designed to meet the objectives of a specific engagement.
Workpapers
The documented record of audit planning, procedures, evidence and conclusions.
Engagement letter
A document that confirms the scope, objectives, timing, responsibilities and access for an audit.
Scope limitation
A restriction on the auditor's access or procedures that prevents gathering sufficient evidence.
Compliance test
A test of whether a control operates as designed, such as checking approvals on a sample of changes.
Substantive test
A test of the data or transactions themselves to detect errors or misstatement.
Attribute sampling
A method that estimates the rate at which a characteristic, such as a control failure, occurs in a population.
Tolerable error rate
The maximum rate of deviation the auditor is willing to accept and still rely on the control.
Sufficient evidence
Enough evidence, in quantity, to support the audit conclusion.
Reperformance
The auditor independently executing a control or calculation to confirm it produces the correct result.
Corroboration
Obtaining additional evidence to confirm information gathered through inquiry or other weaker sources.
Walkthrough
Tracing a single transaction through a process to confirm the auditor's understanding of controls.
CAAT
Computer-assisted audit technique: using software to extract and analyze data or test system logic as part of an audit.
Parallel simulation
Reprocessing production data with auditor-controlled software and comparing the results with the production output.
Embedded audit module
Code inside an application that selects transactions meeting audit criteria as they are processed.
Continuous monitoring
Management's ongoing, automated oversight of controls and key indicators.
Condition
The part of a finding that states what the auditor actually observed.
Criteria
The standard, policy or expectation that the condition is measured against.
Exit meeting
A meeting at the end of fieldwork where findings are discussed with management before the report is finalized.
Follow-up
Audit procedures that verify whether agreed corrective actions were implemented and effective.

Domain 2: Governance and management of IT (18%)

Exam tips

  • The first audit question about a new law is whether management has identified and assessed the requirements. Controls come after that assessment.
  • Governance is the board's job; management executes. If an answer places ultimate accountability with the CIO or IT department, look for the board instead.
  • Policies are approved by senior management and state intent; they should not contain technical detail. If a question asks what the auditor reviews first to understand management's intent, pick the policy.
  • Risk acceptance belongs to a business owner with authority, within the risk appetite. Auditors identify and report risks; they never accept them.
  • Security and privacy overlap but differ. A question about using data for a new purpose or collecting too much is about privacy principles, not encryption.
  • The owner decides and is accountable; the custodian implements. When asked who classifies data or approves access, choose the business owner, not IT or security.
  • The classic IT SoD conflict is the same person developing and moving code to production. If full separation is impossible, look for a compensating detective control done by someone independent.
  • Type 1 means design only at one point; Type 2 adds operating effectiveness over time. An auditor relying on a vendor's controls normally wants Type 2, and must still check complementary user entity controls.
  • Look for measures that show value and outcomes, not just activity. A balanced scorecard that links IT to business goals is usually the best answer for showing IT's contribution.

Key terms

Regulatory compliance
Meeting the requirements imposed by laws and regulations that apply to the organization.
Compliance register
A maintained list of applicable legal, regulatory and contractual requirements with owners and status.
Breach notification
A legal requirement to inform regulators or affected people when certain data is compromised.
Industry standard
A requirement set developed by an industry body, such as for card payments, which may become binding through contracts.
IT governance
The leadership, structures and processes that ensure IT supports the organization's strategy and objectives.
IT steering committee
A management committee of business and IT leaders that prioritizes, approves and monitors IT investments.
Business alignment
The degree to which IT plans and investments support the organization's strategic goals.
IT strategy committee
A board-level committee that advises the board on the strategic direction of IT.
Policy
A high-level, management-approved statement of intent and direction.
Standard
A mandatory, specific requirement that supports a policy.
Procedure
Detailed, step-by-step instructions to perform a task.
COBIT
ISACA's framework for governance and management of enterprise information and technology.
Enterprise architecture
A structured description of business processes, information, applications and technology and how they should evolve.
Risk appetite
The amount and type of risk an organization is willing to pursue or retain to achieve its objectives.
Residual risk
The risk remaining after controls and other responses are applied.
Risk owner
The person accountable for managing a particular risk and deciding on its response.
Purpose limitation
The principle that personal data is used only for the specific purposes for which it was collected.
Data minimization
Collecting and keeping only the personal data that is necessary for the stated purpose.
Privacy impact assessment (PIA)
An evaluation of how a new system or change affects personal data and privacy risk, done before it is implemented.
Privacy by design
Building privacy protections into systems and processes from the start.
Data owner
The business manager accountable for a data set, who decides its classification and approves access.
Data custodian
The party, often IT, that implements and maintains controls over data on the owner's behalf.
Data classification
Assigning data to sensitivity levels that determine how it must be handled and protected.
Data steward
A person responsible for the quality, definitions and proper use of data day to day.
Segregation of duties
Dividing incompatible duties among different people so that no single person can commit and conceal errors or fraud.
Compensating control
An alternative control that reduces risk when the ideal control, such as full segregation of duties, cannot be applied.
Portfolio management
Managing a set of IT investments together to maximize value and alignment within available resources.
Job rotation
Periodically moving staff between roles, which reduces dependence on individuals and can reveal irregularities.
Right-to-audit clause
A contract term allowing the customer, or its auditors, to audit the vendor's controls.
SOC 2 Type 2
An independent report on a service organization's controls over the trust services criteria, including testing of operating effectiveness over a period.
Complementary user entity controls
Controls that the customer must perform for the service organization's controls to be effective.
Source code escrow
An arrangement where a third party holds a vendor's source code, released to the customer if the vendor fails.
Key performance indicator (KPI)
A measure of how well a process or activity is achieving its objective.
Key risk indicator (KRI)
A metric that signals increasing exposure to a particular risk.
Balanced scorecard
A performance tool that measures across financial, customer, internal process and learning and growth perspectives.
Capability level
A rating of how well a process is performed and managed, from incomplete to optimized.

Domain 3: Information systems acquisition, development and implementation (12%)

Exam tips

  • Know the formulas: CV = EV - AC, SV = EV - PV, CPI = EV / AC, SPI = EV / PV. Negative variances or indexes below 1 mean trouble.
  • When a project's costs rise or benefits fall, the best answer is usually to re-evaluate the business case, not to continue because of money already spent.
  • Agile and DevOps do not mean no controls. Look for controls in different places: backlog acceptance criteria, definition of done, automated tests and pipeline approval gates.
  • Match the control to the error: validity or range checks for bad individual values, record counts and hash totals for missing or altered batch items, reconciliations for completeness of output.
  • UAT sign-off belongs to the business users and system owner, not IT. And production data used in testing should be masked to protect privacy.
  • Parallel is lowest risk and highest cost; direct cutover is highest risk and lowest cost. Phased and pilot sit in between.
  • Reconciliation with record counts, control totals and hash totals, signed off by the data owner, is the key data conversion control. Keep the old data until the conversion is verified.
  • The PIR happens after the system has run long enough to measure benefits, not right after go-live and not before. Its main reference point is the business case.

Key terms

Project sponsor
The senior business leader who owns the business case, funds the project and is accountable for its benefits.
Earned value
The budgeted cost of the work actually completed at a point in time.
Cost performance index (CPI)
Earned value divided by actual cost; below 1 means the project is over budget.
Scope creep
Uncontrolled growth in project scope without matching changes in budget, time or approval.
Business case
A document that justifies an investment by comparing options, costs, benefits and risks.
Total cost of ownership (TCO)
All costs of a solution over its life, including acquisition, operation, support and retirement.
Feasibility study
An analysis of whether a proposed solution is technically, economically, operationally and legally practical.
Sunk cost
Money already spent that cannot be recovered and should not drive future decisions.
Waterfall (SDLC)
A sequential development approach where each phase is completed and signed off before the next begins.
Product backlog
A prioritized list of features and requirements, usually written as user stories, used in agile development.
DevOps
Practices that combine development and operations with automation to deliver changes quickly and reliably.
Prototyping
Building an early working model of a system to refine requirements with users.
Validity check
An edit check that accepts only values that are permitted for a field, such as real dates or valid codes.
Hash total
A total of a non-financial field, such as account numbers, used to detect changes or missing items in a batch.
Run-to-run totals
Control totals carried from one processing step to the next to confirm nothing was lost or added.
Check digit
A calculated digit appended to a number that detects transcription errors when the number is entered.
Unit testing
Testing individual program modules in isolation, usually by developers.
User acceptance testing (UAT)
Testing by business users to confirm the system meets their requirements before go-live.
Regression testing
Rerunning previous tests after a change to confirm existing functions still work.
Black-box testing
Testing functionality by checking outputs for given inputs without examining internal code.
Release management
The process of planning, packaging, approving and deploying tested changes into production.
Parallel changeover
Running the old and new systems at the same time and comparing results before switching fully.
Direct cutover
Switching from the old system to the new one at a single point in time with no parallel running.
Rollback plan
A prepared procedure to return to the previous state if a release fails.
Data conversion
Transforming and moving data from an old system's format to a new system's format.
Data mapping
Defining how each field in the source system corresponds to fields in the target system.
Control total
A sum of a numeric field, such as amounts, used to confirm that all data was processed or transferred.
Infrastructure as code
Defining and deploying infrastructure through version-controlled configuration files instead of manual setup.
Post-implementation review (PIR)
A review after go-live that compares actual results with the business case and captures lessons learned.
Benefits realization
Managing and tracking the delivery of the benefits promised by an investment.
Lessons learned
Documented insights from a project that help improve future projects.
Benefit owner
The business person accountable for achieving a specific benefit after implementation.

Domain 4: Information systems operations and business resilience (26%)

Exam tips

  • An accurate asset inventory is the foundation of IT operations and security. When asked what to do first to manage or protect assets, building or verifying the inventory is often the answer.
  • For interfaces, the best completeness control is reconciliation of counts and totals between sending and receiving systems. Encryption protects confidentiality but does not prove completeness.
  • For shadow IT, the best first step is discovery and risk assessment, not blocking everything. For EUC, the key risks are lack of change control, testing, access control and backup.
  • Capacity management is proactive: it uses trends and business forecasts to act before resources run out. Waiting for a failure is the wrong answer.
  • Incident management restores service fast, even with a workaround. Problem management finds and fixes the root cause. Repeated identical incidents point to missing problem management.
  • To find unauthorized changes, sample from what actually changed in production (system logs, version control) and trace back to approvals. Sampling only from approved tickets cannot reveal changes that never had one.
  • The best evidence of SLA performance is reliable, independent measurement compared with targets. For logs, the key concerns are completeness, protection from alteration, adequate retention and actual review.
  • DBAs can bypass application controls. The best answers involve logging DBA activity to a protected location and having someone independent review it.
  • RPO is about data loss and drives backup frequency; RTO is about time to restore and drives recovery site choice. The BIA comes first, before choosing strategies or sites.
  • A successful backup job does not prove recoverability. Periodic restore tests are the best evidence that backups work, and at least one copy should be offsite and protected from ransomware.
  • The BIA comes first, then strategy, plan, testing and maintenance. After a failed test, update the plan and retest; after major changes, update the plan.

Key terms

IT asset management (ITAM)
Tracking and managing IT assets through their lifecycle, from acquisition to disposal.
Software asset management
Managing software licenses and installations to ensure compliance and control costs.
End of life (EOL)
The point after which a vendor no longer supports or updates a product.
Media sanitization
Removing data from storage media so that it cannot be recovered.
Job scheduler
Software that runs batch jobs automatically in a defined order and time.
System interface
A connection that transfers data between two applications, by file or in real time.
Application programming interface (API)
A defined way for software components to request and exchange data with each other.
Robotic process automation (RPA)
Software bots that mimic user actions to automate repetitive tasks.
End-user computing (EUC)
Applications and tools developed or managed by business users outside the formal IT function.
Shadow IT
Technology used in the organization without IT's knowledge or approval.
EUC inventory
A list of end-user tools with their owners, purpose and risk rating.
Cloud access security broker (CASB)
A tool that discovers and controls use of cloud services.
Availability
The proportion of agreed time that a service is usable, often expressed as a percentage.
Capacity management
Ensuring IT resources meet current and future demand cost-effectively.
Mean time between failures (MTBF)
The average operating time between failures of a component or system.
Mean time to repair (MTTR)
The average time needed to restore a failed component or service.
Incident
An unplanned interruption or reduction in the quality of an IT service.
Problem
The underlying, often unknown, cause of one or more incidents.
Root cause analysis
A structured investigation to identify the fundamental cause of a problem.
Known error
A problem whose root cause has been identified and for which a workaround is documented.
Change advisory board (CAB)
A group that assesses and approves or rejects significant changes.
Emergency change
An urgent change implemented quickly, then documented and approved after the fact.
Configuration item
Any component that must be managed to deliver an IT service, recorded with its attributes and relationships.
Patch management
Identifying, testing and deploying software updates that fix vulnerabilities or defects.
Service level agreement (SLA)
An agreement between a service provider and customer that defines measurable service targets.
Operational level agreement (OLA)
An internal agreement between IT teams supporting the delivery of an SLA.
Log retention
How long logs are kept, set by policy, legal requirements and investigation needs.
Time synchronization
Keeping system clocks aligned, for example with NTP, so events can be correlated across systems.
Referential integrity
A rule ensuring that foreign key values always match an existing primary key in the related table.
Normalization
Structuring a database so each fact is stored only once, reducing redundancy and inconsistency.
ACID
Properties of reliable transactions: atomicity, consistency, isolation and durability.
Database activity monitoring
Tools that record and analyze database access and changes, especially by privileged users.
Business impact analysis (BIA)
An analysis of critical processes, their dependencies and the impact of disruption over time.
Recovery time objective (RTO)
The target time within which a system or process must be restored after a disruption.
Recovery point objective (RPO)
The maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured in time before the disruption.
Maximum tolerable downtime (MTD)
The longest time a process can be disrupted before the impact becomes unacceptable.
Incremental backup
A backup of data changed since the last backup of any type.
Differential backup
A backup of data changed since the last full backup.
Immutable backup
A backup copy that cannot be changed or deleted for a set period, protecting against ransomware.
Single point of failure
A component whose failure stops the whole service because it has no redundancy.
Hot site
A fully equipped recovery site with current data that can resume operations within hours.
Cold site
A recovery facility with space and utilities but no equipment or data.
Tabletop exercise
A discussion-based test where participants walk through a scenario to check their understanding of the plan.
Full interruption test
A test that shuts down primary operations and runs entirely at the recovery site; the most realistic and most disruptive.

Domain 5: Protection of information assets (26%)

Exam tips

  • A baseline makes configuration consistent and testable, but it does not eliminate the need for vulnerability scanning or monitoring. Exceptions must be formal, approved and time-limited.
  • A camera or sign-in sheet detects or deters; a mantrap prevents tailgating. For staffed data centers, pre-action sprinklers balance safety and water damage risk.
  • Owners review access; administrators implement. For leavers, the best control links HR terminations to automatic account removal, supported by periodic reviews.
  • IDS detects and alerts; IPS sits inline and blocks. Firewall rule sets should deny by default, and rules near the top that allow any traffic defeat everything below them.
  • Encryption is only as good as its key management. A key stored next to the data it protects is a common finding.
  • Sign with your private key; verify with the sender's public key. Encrypt for confidentiality with the recipient's public key. Symmetric keys cannot give non-repudiation because both sides hold them.
  • In the cloud, the customer always keeps accountability for its data, identities and configuration. Most cloud breaches come from customer misconfiguration, not provider failure.
  • Measure awareness by behavior change, such as phishing simulation click and report trends, rather than attendance. For payment fraud, independent call-back verification is a strong process control.
  • A vulnerability scan identifies; a penetration test exploits to prove impact. No penetration test should start without written authorization and agreed rules of engagement.
  • A SIEM with missing log sources or unreviewed alerts gives false comfort. Coverage, tuning and evidence that alerts are investigated are what make monitoring effective.
  • Collect evidence in order of volatility, work on verified copies and keep chain of custody. Before restoring after ransomware, make sure the attacker's access is removed and backups are clean.

Key terms

Information security management system (ISMS)
A systematic approach to managing information security risks through policies, processes and controls, as defined in ISO/IEC 27001.
Security baseline
The minimum set of security settings required for a type of system.
Policy exception
A formally approved and time-limited deviation from a policy or standard, with an accountable owner.
Hardening
Reducing a system's attack surface by removing unneeded services and applying secure settings.
Mantrap
An entry area with two doors that lets only one authenticated person pass at a time, preventing tailgating.
Tailgating
Following an authorized person through a secured door without authenticating.
Pre-action sprinkler
A sprinkler system whose pipes stay dry until a detector triggers, reducing accidental water damage.
Uninterruptible power supply (UPS)
A battery-based device that provides short-term power and conditioning during outages.
Least privilege
Granting users only the minimum access needed to perform their duties.
Multifactor authentication (MFA)
Authentication that combines two or more different factor types.
Privilege creep
Gradual accumulation of access rights as users change roles without removing old access.
Access recertification
A periodic review in which owners confirm that users' access remains appropriate.
Network segmentation
Dividing a network into zones with controlled traffic between them to limit the spread of attacks.
Intrusion prevention system (IPS)
An inline device that detects and blocks malicious network traffic.
Endpoint detection and response (EDR)
Software that monitors endpoint behavior to detect, investigate and contain threats.
Demilitarized zone (DMZ)
A network segment that hosts internet-facing services, separated from the internal network.
Data loss prevention (DLP)
Tools and processes that detect and prevent unauthorized transmission or storage of sensitive data.
Symmetric encryption
Encryption that uses the same secret key to encrypt and decrypt.
Key management
Controls over the generation, storage, distribution, rotation and destruction of cryptographic keys.
Hardware security module (HSM)
A tamper-resistant device that securely generates, stores and uses cryptographic keys.
Certificate authority (CA)
A trusted entity that issues and signs digital certificates.
Digital signature
A hash of a message encrypted with the signer's private key, providing integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.
Certificate revocation list (CRL)
A list published by a CA of certificates revoked before their expiry.
Non-repudiation
Assurance that a party cannot credibly deny having sent or signed a message.
Shared responsibility model
The division of security duties between a cloud provider and its customer, which varies by service model.
Hypervisor
Software that creates and runs virtual machines on a physical host.
Mobile device management (MDM)
Tools that enforce security policies on mobile devices, including encryption and remote wipe.
Internet of Things (IoT)
Network-connected physical devices such as sensors, cameras and controllers.
Phishing
Deceptive messages that try to trick recipients into revealing information or running malicious content.
Business email compromise (BEC)
Fraud using spoofed or compromised business email to trick staff into sending money or data.
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts or steals data and demands payment for its release.
SQL injection
An attack that inserts malicious database commands through poorly validated input.
Vulnerability scan
An automated check that identifies known weaknesses on systems and applications.
Penetration test
An authorized, controlled attempt to exploit vulnerabilities to demonstrate real impact.
Rules of engagement
The agreed scope, timing, methods, contacts and limits for a security test.
Authenticated scan
A vulnerability scan that logs in to systems for more complete and accurate results.
SIEM
Security information and event management: a system that collects, correlates and analyzes security events and raises alerts.
Correlation rule
Logic that links related events from different sources to identify suspicious patterns.
Alert fatigue
Desensitization caused by too many alerts, especially false positives, leading to missed real threats.
Security operations center (SOC)
A team that monitors, triages and responds to security alerts.
Containment
Actions that limit the spread and impact of an incident.
Order of volatility
The sequence for collecting evidence, from most short-lived (memory) to most persistent (archives).
Chain of custody
Documentation showing who handled evidence, when and how, to prove it was not altered.
Forensic image
An exact bit-for-bit copy of storage media, verified with a hash, used for analysis.
