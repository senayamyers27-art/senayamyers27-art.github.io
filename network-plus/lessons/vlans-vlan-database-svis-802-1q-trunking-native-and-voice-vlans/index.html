Explain it simpler

A switch normally puts every plugged-in device into one big shared neighborhood where everyone hears everyone's announcements. A VLAN (virtual LAN) lets you draw invisible fences, so one physical switch acts like several separate switches: one for staff, one for guests, one for phones. Devices in different fenced areas cannot talk unless a router lets them. When fenced areas need to stretch across two switches, the cable between them becomes a trunk, and each piece of traffic gets a small label, called a tag, saying which area it belongs to. Think of an apartment building's shared elevator: everyone rides the same elevator, but the button you press decides which floor you reach.

A ticket lands in your queue at Brightwater Elementary: the new classroom wing is open, the projectors work, the teachers' phones ring, but the teachers' laptops plugged into the back of those same phones get no network at all. Meanwhile, the guest Wi-Fi on the second floor hands out no addresses, and the core switch log keeps printing a warning about a native VLAN mismatch. Jonah, the district technician, installed the new floor switch yesterday and swears he copied the old configuration. One cable, one port, three different symptoms. What did he miss?

A virtual LAN (VLAN) divides one physical switch, or a group of switches, into separate logical Layer 2 networks. Each VLAN is its own broadcast domain and normally its own IP subnet. VLANs let you group users by function rather than location, contain broadcast traffic, and separate traffic for security, for example keeping guest, staff, voice, camera and management traffic apart. Devices in different VLANs cannot talk to each other without a router or Layer 3 switch, which gives you a natural point to apply access control lists (ACLs) that decide what may cross between them.
