CompTIA Network+ N10-009 · Domain 1: Networking concepts

Protocol types (TCP, UDP, ICMP, GRE, IPsec) and traffic types (unicast, multicast, anycast, broadcast)

Explain it simpler

Different kinds of network traffic need different delivery styles. TCP is like a phone call: both sides say hello first, confirm every part was heard, and repeat anything missed. UDP is like a postcard: you send it and hope it arrives, which is quick and fine for live audio where a late piece is useless anyway. ICMP is the network's way of sending error notes, like 'cannot deliver' or 'took too long,' and ping uses it. GRE wraps one packet inside another, like putting a letter inside a bigger envelope, and IPsec locks that envelope so nobody can read or change it. Traffic can also go to one receiver (unicast), everyone nearby (broadcast), a group that signed up (multicast), or the nearest of several copies (anycast).

Owen, the network engineer at Silverline Logistics, has just brought up a new tunnel between the main warehouse and a distribution center. Pings cross the tunnel fine, yet the routing protocol refuses to share routes, and the security team has already emailed: 'Is that tunnel encrypted? Auditors will ask.' Meanwhile a firewall on the far end sits behind a NAT router, and the VPN keeps failing at that site only. Three protocols with similar-sounding jobs are involved, and each one does something the others cannot. Which protocol carries the routing updates, which one keeps them secret, and why would the same VPN work at one site and fail behind NAT at another?

Above IP, different protocols carry data in different ways, and choosing or permitting the right one is a daily networking task. The IP header has a protocol field that tells the receiver what comes next: 6 for TCP, 17 for UDP, 1 for ICMP, 47 for GRE, 50 for ESP and 51 for AH. You rarely need these numbers on the exam, but knowing that ICMP, GRE and IPsec's ESP and AH sit directly on IP, with no port numbers, explains why firewalls and NAT devices must treat them differently from ordinary TCP and UDP traffic.
