IPv6: address types, dual stack, tunneling, NAT64

Explain it simpler

The original internet address system, IPv4, ran out of addresses, so a much bigger system called IPv6 was created. IPv6 addresses are long, written as eight groups of letters and numbers separated by colons, and there are rules to shorten them by dropping zeros. The first few characters tell you what kind of address it is, like an area code: fe80 means 'only works on this local network,' 2 or 3 means a public internet address, and ff means a group message. Devices can build their own IPv6 address from information routers announce. Because IPv4 and IPv6 will both be around for years, networks run both at once, carry IPv6 inside IPv4 when needed, or translate between them, like an interpreter between two languages.

A ticket comes in at Summit Valley College: a lab PC in the engineering building 'has a weird address and cannot get anywhere.' Lucia, the student worker, has pasted the output of ipconfig: no IPv4 address at all, just a long string beginning fe80:: on the Ethernet adapter. Meanwhile, the campus is rolling out an IPv6-only Wi-Fi network for phones, and someone in the dean's office asks how those phones will reach an old library catalog that only speaks IPv4. You have seen IPv6 addresses before, but they look like noise. In a moment you will learn to read that first hextet like a label. What does fe80 tell you, and why does that PC stop at its own switch?

IPv6 was created mainly because IPv4's roughly 4.3 billion addresses ran out. An IPv6 address is 128 bits, written as eight groups (hextets) of four hexadecimal digits separated by colons, such as 2001:0db8:0000:0000:0000:ff00:0042:8329. That space is so large that NAT (Network Address Translation) is no longer needed to conserve addresses, and every device can have a globally unique address. IPv6 also simplifies the header, removes broadcast and builds in autoconfiguration. Network+ expects you to read and shorten addresses, recognize address types by their prefix, and know how IPv6 coexists with IPv4.
