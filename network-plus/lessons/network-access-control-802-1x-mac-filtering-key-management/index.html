CompTIA Network+ N10-009 · Domain 4: Network security

Network access control: 802.1X, MAC filtering, key management

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 6, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Network access control (NAC) is a gatekeeper that decides who or what may join the network before letting it in. The strongest common method is called 802.1X. When you plug in or join Wi-Fi, the switch or access point holds the door shut and asks a central server to check your username, password or digital certificate. If you pass, you are let in, sometimes into a specific section of the network. A weaker method, MAC filtering, only checks a device's hardware address, which is easy to fake, like a guest list that checks names but not IDs. Key management means looking after the passwords, keys and certificates behind all this: creating strong ones, storing them safely, changing them on schedule and renewing them before they expire.

At 7:30 a.m. on the first day of term, the help desk at Summit State University lights up: nobody on campus Wi-Fi or in the labs can connect, yet the printers are working fine. Theo, the network engineer, checks the switches and access points; all are healthy. Then he opens the RADIUS server and sees one line repeated thousands of times: the client rejected the server certificate. Somewhere, a calendar reminder about a renewal date was missed. Last year, the campus ran on a single shared Wi-Fi password and a list of allowed MAC addresses, and people wonder aloud whether that was simpler. Why did printers keep working, and why is a certificate the reason everyone else cannot get in?

Network access control (NAC) decides whether a device may connect to the network, and to which part, before it gets access. Instead of trusting anything plugged into a wall jack or joined to Wi-Fi, NAC checks who or what the device is, and often whether it meets security policy, then allows, restricts or denies it. It turns every access port and wireless network name (SSID) into a checkpoint, which supports zero trust and segmentation. Network+ focuses on 802.1X, the weak but common alternative of MAC filtering, and the key management that keeps credentials and encryption trustworthy.
