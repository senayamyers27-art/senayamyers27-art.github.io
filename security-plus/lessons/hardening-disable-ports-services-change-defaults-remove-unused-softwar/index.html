CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 · Domain 2: Threats, vulnerabilities & mitigations

Hardening: disable ports/services, change defaults, remove unused software

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 6, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Hardening means making a device or system harder to attack by removing anything unnecessary and fixing weak settings. That includes turning off network ports and services that are not needed, since each one is a possible way in. It also means changing default settings and passwords, because manufacturers ship devices with well-known defaults that attackers look up online. Removing software that is not used shrinks the number of things that could have flaws. The goal is to reduce the attack surface, which is the total set of ways in. Think of moving into a new house: you change the locks the builder used, you board up a basement door you never use, and you throw out the spare keys left under the mat.

Facilities at Bayview Community College just installed forty new security cameras, and Sam from IT is asked to sign off before they go live. Out of curiosity, Sam runs a quick scan of one camera on the test bench. It answers on a web page, on Telnet, and on a port the manual never mentions. The login is printed on a sticker on the box: admin, with the password admin. Sam knows automated scanners on the internet try exactly that combination thousands of times a day. The cameras work perfectly, and that is the problem: they were built to be easy to set up, not hard to break into. What has to happen before any of them touch the network?

Hardening is the process of reducing a system's attack surface by removing or disabling everything it does not need and securing what remains. Systems usually ship with convenience in mind: many services enabled, default accounts and passwords, sample files and extra software. Each of those is a potential entry point that the owner did not choose and may not know about. Hardening applies to servers, workstations, network devices, mobile devices, cloud resources, databases and embedded or IoT (Internet of Things) devices, and Security+ expects you to know the common steps and why each one matters.
