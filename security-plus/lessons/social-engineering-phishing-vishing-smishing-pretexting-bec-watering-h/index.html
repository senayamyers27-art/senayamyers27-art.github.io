CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 · Domain 2: Threats, vulnerabilities & mitigations

Social engineering: phishing, vishing, smishing, pretexting, BEC, watering hole, typosquatting

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 6, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Social engineering means tricking people instead of breaking technology. Phishing uses fake emails to get clicks or passwords; vishing does it by phone and smishing by text. Pretexting means inventing a believable story, like pretending to be IT support, to get information. Business email compromise (BEC) uses a real or faked company email account, often an executive's, to request payments or data. A watering hole attack infects a website that a target group often visits. Typosquatting registers web addresses with common misspellings of real ones, so a typo lands you on a fake site. All of them exploit trust, urgency, or fear. It is like a con artist in a delivery uniform who says your boss urgently needs the safe opened before 5 p.m.

It is 4:40 on a Friday afternoon at Ridgeway Home Builders, and Dana in accounts payable is trying to clear her queue before the weekend. An email arrives from the company's longtime lumber supplier: their bank has changed, and this week's payment must go to the new account today or deliveries will stop. Two minutes later her phone rings. A friendly voice says he is the supplier's accountant, apologizes for the rush and offers to stay on the line while she updates the details. The logo is right, the invoice number is right, and the caller knows her name. Nothing about this involves malware. So what should make Dana stop?

Social engineering targets people rather than technology. It manipulates people into doing something that helps an attacker: revealing a password, approving a payment, opening a file or holding a door. It works because it targets human instincts rather than technical flaws, so a fully patched network can still be breached through one convincing email. Security+ expects you to recognize each technique by its channel and method, and to know which controls reduce the risk. Most real breaches involve a human element at some point, which is why this topic is always well represented on the exam.
