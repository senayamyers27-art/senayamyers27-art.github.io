CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 · Domain 1: General security concepts

ARP, DNS, DHCP and their attacks

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 6, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Three basic network services keep a local network running, and each can be abused. ARP matches a device's IP address to its hardware address on the local network; in ARP poisoning, an attacker falsely claims to be another device, such as the router, so traffic flows through them. DNS turns names like a website address into numeric IP addresses; in DNS poisoning or spoofing, an attacker supplies fake answers that send people to the wrong site. DHCP hands out IP addresses and network settings automatically; a rogue DHCP server gives bad settings that point devices at an attacker, and DHCP starvation uses up all the addresses. It is like someone swapping the labels on office mailboxes, giving out a fake phone directory, or posing as the front desk handing out wrong room numbers.

On Tuesday morning the help desk at Northgate Community College gets six tickets in twenty minutes, all from the second floor of the library: every website shows a certificate warning. Upstairs, nothing looks broken. The Wi-Fi works, the printers print, and students are starting to click 'continue anyway' just to finish their assignments. Down the hall, Theo from the network team pulls up one affected laptop and notices that the default gateway's hardware address looks unfamiliar. Is the problem the browser, the name lookups, the address handed out when the laptop joined, or something sitting quietly between the students and the router?

Three quiet protocols make every network work, and all three were designed with little built-in security. The Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) finds the hardware address of a device on the local network, the Domain Name System (DNS) turns names into IP addresses, and the Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) hands out network settings. Each one trusts whoever answers, so an attacker who can answer first, or answer falsely, can redirect traffic. Understanding how they normally work makes their attacks easy to recognize, and Security+ regularly asks you to match symptoms to the right attack and the right defense.
