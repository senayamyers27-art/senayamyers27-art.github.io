CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 · Domain 1: General security concepts

Revocation: CRL vs OCSP, OCSP stapling

▶ Watch the overview video

Last reviewed October 6, 2026 · Leer en español

Explain it simpler

Sometimes a certificate must be cancelled before it expires, for example if its private key is stolen. This is called revocation. A certificate revocation list (CRL) is a published list of cancelled certificates that browsers download and check, but it can be large and out of date between updates. OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol) lets a browser ask the CA in real time whether one specific certificate is still good. OCSP stapling has the website itself fetch a recent signed answer from the CA and attach it when visitors connect, which is faster and keeps the CA from seeing every visitor. Think of checking a credit card: a printed list of stolen card numbers is like a CRL, calling the bank is like OCSP, and the customer showing a fresh bank-stamped note is like stapling.

At 2:10 a.m. your phone buzzes. You are on call for Coastal Freight's infrastructure team, and the alert says a backup archive containing the private key for the customer portal was found in a publicly readable storage bucket. The certificate is valid for another eight months. You picture someone standing up a lookalike portal with your real certificate and a reassuring padlock icon. You can generate a new key pair in minutes, but that alone does nothing to stop anyone using the old one. How do you tell browsers everywhere to stop trusting a certificate that has not expired, and how quickly will they actually notice?

Certificates have an expiration date, but sometimes a certificate must stop being trusted before then. The private key may have been stolen, the domain sold, the employee who held the certificate may have left, or the certificate authority (CA) may have issued it by mistake. Revocation is how a CA announces 'do not trust this certificate any more'. It matters because a stolen private key paired with a still-trusted certificate lets an attacker impersonate a site or person perfectly until the certificate is revoked and clients actually check. Security+ tests the two main checking methods and the improvement called OCSP stapling. A related idea is certificate suspension, sometimes called a hold, which temporarily marks a certificate as untrusted and can later be lifted, whereas revocation is permanent.
