Explain it simpler

The power supply is the metal box inside a computer that takes electricity from the wall and turns it into the gentler kind of power the parts need. Wall power changes direction many times a second and is fairly high voltage; computer parts want steady, low voltages such as 12, 5 and 3.3 volts. Different countries supply different wall voltages, so a power supply must be able to handle the local level. Its wattage number says how much power it can deliver in total, and if the parts ask for more, the computer can switch off suddenly. It is like the water pressure regulator on a house: it takes the high pressure from the street and delivers a safe, steady flow to each tap.

Ana, a researcher at Lakeshore Institute, has just returned from a six-month field placement overseas, where she used her office desktop on local wall power. Back home, the PC will not start at all. Meanwhile, down the hall, a gamer on the IT team, Theo, has fitted a much bigger graphics card into his own machine, and now it reboots every time a game gets busy, though web browsing is fine. And in the server room, the storage server's front panel shows one amber light that nobody has explained. Three different machines, three different symptoms, and every one of them comes back to the same box at the back of the case. What is that box doing, and how do you read what it is telling you?

The power supply unit (PSU) converts alternating current (AC) from the wall outlet into the low-voltage direct current (DC) that computer components use. A weak or failing PSU causes random shutdowns, reboots and failures to start, and those symptoms are easy to blame on other parts, so choosing and checking the right PSU is an important skill. Never open a PSU: its capacitors can hold a dangerous charge even when unplugged. Failed units are replaced, not repaired.
